  1. Set Number of subjects to how many you want to compare, from 1 to 6. A named column appears for each.

  3. Score every goal from 0 to 5 in each column. The chart redraws as you type.
  4. Use Paste values to fill a whole column at once — pick the target column first, then paste and press Read values.
  5. Press Download PNG to save the chart for a report or course file.

What the 0–5 score means

The scale is relative, not absolute. If you are deriving scores from a project-by-project SDG matrix, the usual method is: count the projects marking each goal primary (P) and secondary (S), compute a weighted count as P + 0.5 × S, then normalise so the highest-scoring goal becomes 5 — round(5 × weighted ÷ max weighted). Because of that normalisation some goal always reaches 5. Two charts therefore compare shape, not magnitude.

Accepted paste formats

One goal per line, where the leading number is the goal and the last number on the line is the score:

1: No Poverty	0
2: Zero Hunger	1
3: Good Health and Well-being	4

Or 17 plain numbers separated by commas, spaces or newlines: 0, 1, 4, 4, 0, 1, 0, 2, 5, 3, 5, 1, 2, 0, 0, 0, 0. Any goal you leave out is set to 0.

Notes

  • Values outside 0–5 are clamped; decimals are rounded; blank cells count as 0.
  • Each subject gets its own colour and dash pattern, so the chart stays readable in greyscale print.
  • The axis is fixed at 0–5 rather than auto-scaling, so charts made at different times stay directly comparable.
  • Nothing is saved — values live in the page only. Download the PNG, or keep the numbers in your spreadsheet.
  • The page loads Chart.js from a CDN, so it needs an internet connection the first time it opens.

About SDG mapping

What the SDGs are

The Sustainable Development Goals are seventeen global priorities adopted by every United Nations member state in 2015, covering poverty, health, education, equality, water, energy, work, industry, cities, consumption, climate, oceans, land, justice and partnership.

For an engineer they serve a narrower purpose: they are a shared vocabulary for stating who a piece of technical work is meant to benefit. A project described only in technical terms — a sensor, a mechanism, a control loop — says nothing about why it should exist. Naming the goal it serves supplies that missing half of the statement.

Primary and secondary SDGs

Most projects touch several goals, but not equally. The distinction separates purpose from consequence.

Primary (P)Secondary (S)
DefinitionThe goal the project is deliberately designed to advanceA goal it also contributes to, as a by-product of its design
The testRemove this goal and the project loses its reason to existRemove this goal and the project still stands
How manyOne or two per projectZero to three per project
Example
wheelchair transport robot		SDG 3 health access, SDG 10 inclusion of people with mobility impairmentSDG 9 the engineering itself, SDG 11 accessible facilities

Without the distinction, teams claim every goal that sounds plausible and the mapping becomes meaningless — a habit sometimes called SDG-washing. Forcing a choice makes the team articulate what the project is actually for, and it allows honest aggregation: a primary contribution is normally weighted 1 and a secondary 0.5, so a project that merely brushes a goal does not count the same as one built around it.

SDG targets

Beneath the seventeen goals sit 169 numbered targets — the specific commitments that give each goal meaning. Citing the target rather than the goal is more precise and far easier to defend.

FormMeaningExample
3.8 numericAn outcome target — what should change in the worldAchieve universal health coverage and access to essential health services
9.c letteredA means-of-implementation target — the finance, technology or capacity neededSignificantly increase access to information and communications technology

Compare the two statements. “This project addresses SDG 3” says almost nothing, since SDG 3 spans maternal mortality, epidemics, road deaths and tobacco. “This project addresses target 3.8, access to essential health services in underserved areas” is a claim that can be examined and either supported or rejected.

Choose targets that genuinely match. A railway inspection robot does not address 3.6 (road traffic deaths) however close it sounds; a project improving nutritional quality fits 2.2 (end malnutrition) rather than 2.1 (access to food). A wrong target is worse than no target — it signals the mapping was not thought through.

Why identify SDGs in a project

  • Accreditation requires it. Under the Washington Accord graduate attributes, PO6 — The Engineer and the World — requires students to analyse and evaluate sustainable development impacts on society, the economy, health and safety, legal frameworks and the environment. An SDG mapping is the most direct evidence a course file can offer. Knowledge profiles WK5 and WK7 are evidenced the same way.
  • It improves the project. Deciding on a primary goal forces the team to answer who benefits and how much — a question that routinely exposes a weak problem statement while there is still time to fix it.
  • It is required outside the college. National innovation competitions organise their themes around the goals, a growing number of journals ask authors to tag submissions by SDG, and funding proposals almost always ask.
  • It gives the department a picture of itself. Aggregated across a batch, the mapping shows where the department concentrates and which goals it never touches — direct input to next year's topic selection.

Why a radar chart suits this data

SDG coverage is seventeen numbers that belong together, and a radar chart is one of the few forms that shows all seventeen at once as a single readable object.

  • It shows profile, not just values. The eye reads the overall shape immediately — whether a department is concentrated in two or three goals or spread thinly across many.
  • It supports comparison. Two or three profiles on the same axes make differences obvious in a way separate bar charts do not. Comparing batches year on year shows drift in emphasis.
  • It makes gaps visible. Spokes that stay at the centre are as informative as the peaks — they identify goals never addressed.

Its limits, worth understanding before using it:

  • The scale is usually relative. When scores are normalised so the strongest goal becomes 5, some goal always reaches the outer ring — charts compare shape, not magnitude.
  • Enclosed area is not a meaningful quantity. It changes with the order of the spokes, which is arbitrary, so area should never be read as total contribution.
  • It is poor for a single project. One project maps two to four goals, giving a few spikes against a flat field; a bar chart of just the mapped goals communicates that better.

In practice

  1. State the problem and who it serves, before looking at the goal list.
  2. Choose one or two primary goals — the ones the project exists to advance.
  3. Add secondary goals only where the contribution is real, not merely plausible.
  4. Name the specific targets under each goal, and check the wording actually matches.
  5. Write one sentence on how the project contributes. If that sentence is hard to write, the mapping is probably wrong.
  6. Record it in the department SDG matrix so it aggregates into the batch profile.
